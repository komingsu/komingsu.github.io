"Turn the learning rate up and it diverges; turn it down and it crawls." Almost everyone
hits this wall early. The usual reading is that the learning rate was chosen badly. Far
more often, the **shape of the loss surface** had already narrowed the range of learning
rates that could possibly work.

This post is about getting a feel for that shape.

## The problem is the condition number

Start with the simplest quadratic:

$$
f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(a x^2 + b y^2\right)
$$

Its gradient is $\nabla f = (a x,\; b y)$, and gradient descent updates each coordinate
independently:

$$
x_{t+1} = (1 - \eta a)\, x_t, \qquad y_{t+1} = (1 - \eta b)\, y_t
$$

The coordinates never interact — that is the whole point. Each shrinks by a factor of
$|1 - \eta a|$ and $|1 - \eta b|$ per step, so convergence needs both factors below one.

<MarginNote counter="†">
  With $a$ the largest curvature and $b$ the smallest, $\kappa = a/b$ is the **condition
  number**. In deep networks it routinely exceeds $10^4$.
</MarginNote>

Take $a > b$. The stability limit is set by the *large* curvature: $\eta < 2/a$. But the
convergence *rate* is set by the small one — $y$ only shrinks by $(1 - \eta b)$ per step.
So **the steepest direction caps the learning rate, and the flattest direction sets the
actual speed.** The worse the ratio between them, the narrower the usable window.

<Callout kind="math" title="In short">
  Step size is bounded by the maximum curvature; time-to-converge is bounded by the
  minimum. As $\kappa$ grows, those two constraints pull apart, and every learning rate
  is a bad compromise for one of them.
</Callout>

Things worth trying:

- **Ravine with momentum 0.9**: the oscillating direction cancels itself out while the
  consistent one accumulates.
- **Rosenbrock at a high learning rate**: on a *curved* valley momentum overshoots into
  the wall. Inertia is not free.
- **Saddle point**: notice how long SGD loiters where the gradient nearly vanishes. Adam's
  shrinking denominator gets it out sooner.
- **Double well, moving the start**: the destination changes. This is what "initialization
  matters" looks like on a non-convex problem.

## What momentum actually does

Momentum<Cite id="polyak1964" refs={frontmatter.references} /> adds one velocity term:

$$
v_{t+1} = \beta v_t - \eta \nabla f(x_t), \qquad x_{t+1} = x_t + v_{t+1}
$$

In a coordinate whose gradient flips sign every step, the terms of $v$ cancel. In one
whose direction is consistent, they accumulate like a geometric series, pushing the
effective learning rate up to $\eta / (1 - \beta)$. At $\beta = 0.9$ that is roughly a
10× boost along the flat direction.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why 0.9">
  Read $1/(1-\beta)$ as the effective multiplier and 0.9 simply means "10×". 0.99 means
  100×, at the cost of turning much more slowly. Push momentum to 0.99 on Rosenbrock above
  and the cost is immediately visible.
</Callout>

Adam<Cite id="kingma2015" refs={frontmatter.references} /> attacks the other side: it
divides each coordinate's step by a running second moment of its gradient, automatically
shrinking steps along high-curvature directions. That is conditioning solved by rescaling
the surface rather than by remembering where you came from.

Goh's spectral treatment<Cite id="goh2017" refs={frontmatter.references} /> is still the
best explanation of momentum, and the optimal acceleration rate is
Nesterov's<Cite id="nesterov1983" refs={frontmatter.references} />.

## Caveats

Every surface above is two-dimensional and noiseless. Real training adds mini-batch noise,
and once the dimension passes $10^8$ it is fair to ask how much the "valley" picture still
buys you. Still — when someone asks why the loss spikes after they raised the learning
rate, this is the first thing to check.
